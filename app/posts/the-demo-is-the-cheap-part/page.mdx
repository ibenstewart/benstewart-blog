<Lede>It's Friday night and I should be switching off. Instead I'm typing into Claude Code, about a website I finished redesigning a month ago. "Why don't we spend some of tonight, and offer me some complete redesigns?"</Lede>

I didn't need one. Glasgow Civic was about a month old and I liked it. I think I wanted to ask for the thing you'd be too embarrassed to ask a designer for, which is to bin the lot and show me three different sites by morning.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-1.jpg" alt="The Glasgow Civic homepage: a pale page with a blue pill navigation, a very large Ben Stewart headline, a photo of Ben speaking into a microphone and a short introduction." caption="What I was offering to bin. Glasgow Civic, a month old." />

It didn't take until morning. Before I went to bed there were three of them on a preview link, each one built out of every post I've ever published here. I sat there clicking round my own writing in three different outfits.

That part took one evening. Picking one took three days. Shipping it properly took more work than building all three, and that's the part worth telling you about.

## Three sites before bed

**Atlas** treated the blog as data. Twenty-five posts and 22,548 words, counted, charted and laid out along a river of dates. It was gorgeous, and it was mostly about me. You'd admire it once and never come back.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-2.jpg" alt="The Atlas homepage: a huge headline reading 25 posts, 7 years, five things I keep coming back to, over a row of stats for words written, minutes of reading, posts and the longest post, with a filter for the five themes." caption="Atlas. Every word I've written, counted." />

**Broadsheet** turned me into a newspaper. My name across the top in type big enough to announce a war, a ticker of my own quotes running underneath, the old Substack posts filed as back issues. Great fun. I'd have hated it by Christmas.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-3.jpg" alt="The Broadsheet homepage: a serif newspaper masthead reading Ben Stewart, a section bar of the five themes, a black ticker of pull quotes and the latest post set as the lead story with a drop cap." caption="Broadsheet. It had a ticker. Of me." />

**Field manual** didn't open with me at all. It opened with a question. What are you dealing with today? Something's on fire. I've just become a manager. I talk too much in meetings. Pick one and it hands you three things to read, in order, with a line on why each one earns its place. The frameworks I keep banging on about turned into cards you can flip over.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-4.jpg" alt="The Field manual homepage as first built: a big question asking what are you dealing with today, a list of situations with Something's on fire selected, and a blue panel showing its three-post reading order." caption="Field manual, the night it was built. Keep an eye on the top right corner." />

It was the only one of the three built for the person reading it. I'd love to tell you that's why I chose it.

## I chose it because of a footer

I spent the weekend not deciding. On Monday I did something else instead, which was to dig out four illustrations I'd saved, all in the same deep blue and cream. Riders crossing a desert of mesas. Torii gates in snow. Chess pieces half buried in sand. Cows in a meadow. Could one of them be a footer?

Claude mocked up all four. Then it asked me something I hadn't asked myself. Where did these come from? The filenames looked like they'd been saved off X, and if I couldn't say who drew them, I couldn't ship them.

I picked the riders anyway and tried them under all three designs. Under two of them they were a nice picture at the bottom of a page. Under Field manual they were the cover. A handful of people on horseback heading out across open country, and above them a book of notes on what to do when it goes wrong.

That was the decision, made by a picture I didn't own.

<Callout type="story">
Somewhere a design lead is wincing. I chose the direction of an entire site from the bottom up, starting with the last thing anybody sees.
</Callout>

The question hadn't gone anywhere, though. The riders belong to an illustrator called [@uixhassan](https://x.com/uixhassan), and I hadn't asked him. So I asked Claude to draw me something of my own in the same spirit. About ten minutes later I had a Highland glen, with four walkers and a dog on a path to a bothy and a loch behind it, every line of it written in code.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-6.jpg" alt="The glen footer: Ben Stewart and the Field manual badge over a blue sky, above an engraved-style Highland glen with walkers on a path towards a small bothy by a loch." caption="The glen. Drawn in code, and fine." />

It went live with the site and it was fine. Fine is a terrible thing for a picture to be. Every time I scrolled to the bottom of my own website I could hear a covers band playing a song I love. I asked for better mountains, proper Scottish ones, and got these.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-7.jpg" alt="The Scottish mountains redraw: tall white peaks with vertical streaks rising out of flat ground, a stag and walkers in the foreground. They look more like icebergs than hills." caption="Proper Scottish mountains, apparently." />

So I did what I should have done on Monday morning, and asked him. He said yes. The riders are back, with his name underneath them. I still owe him a pint.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-8.jpg" alt="The footer as it is now: riders on horseback crossing a desert of blue mesas, with the site links above and a credit line reading Illustration by @uixhassan." caption="Back where they belong." />

## The squiggle

Building it for real meant every post got a number. A chapter for its theme and a place in that chapter by date, so the one about burning out became entry 5.2. I liked that more than I expected to. Years of scattered posts suddenly read like they'd been one book all along.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-9.jpg" alt="The finished Field manual homepage: the question what are you dealing with today, I'm new here selected, and a blue panel with a three-entry reading order above a slider for how many minutes you've got." caption="The real thing. Pick your problem, get three entries in order." />

Then I noticed a little mark in front of every number, and sent a message I was slightly embarrassed to send.

"Are these icons before numbers an error? §1"

They weren't an error. It's the section sign, the squiggle lawyers put in front of clauses. Claude had reached for it because manuals have sections. It was correct, and it was no use to anyone, including the man whose name was on the site.

<Figure screenshot src="/images/posts/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part-5.jpg" alt="Two versions of the navigation. Above, the first build reads §1 situations, §2 toolkit, §3 chapters. Below, the live site reads situations, toolkit, chapters, all entries, speaking, bio." caption="Before and after. Nobody should need a law degree to use a menu." />

If I had to ask, you'd have had to ask. They're plain numbers now.

<KeyPoint>A design that needs explaining has already failed. The cheapest usability test you'll ever run is being confused by your own website.</KeyPoint>

## The demo is the cheap part

By now I was in love with it, which is the worst possible time to ask a sensible question. I asked anyway. What would it take to do this to the whole site, properly? Accessible, tested, all of it.

The answer was a list, and the list was humbling. The thing I'd been showing off had problems that never turn up in a screenshot:

- The amber I liked for one chapter had a contrast of about 2:1 against the page. Readable text needs more than twice that.
- The flip cards kept their backs on the page while hidden, so anyone using a keyboard would tab onto a button they couldn't see.
- A little animation of keys being pressed looped forever, with no way to make it stop.

None of those would ever have bothered me. Every one of them would have bothered somebody.

Fixing them took eight rounds of changes, and left behind more than 80 unit tests and more than 90 browser and accessibility checks that hadn't existed the week before. They paid for themselves on the first run, which found grey text too faint to read at the end of every post and every page sliding sideways on the smallest phones. Then the build server caught one that wouldn't even load on the machine it was written on; the Follow button on an embedded tweet was too small for a thumb.

One job was left that I couldn't hand over. Every situation, every line on a tool card and every chapter blurb is written as me, and I'm the only person who can say whether it sounds like me. So I read all of it.

That's the bit nobody screenshots, and it's most of the bloody work.

I [wrote a while ago](/posts/ai-made-writing-free) that AI didn't make writing free; it moved the cost from the writer to the reader. Design has gone the same way. Three redesigns in an evening didn't make a redesign cheap. The cost moved out of the making and into the choosing, then out of the choosing and into the checking. Making one is nearly free now. Knowing which one, and whether it's actually any good, costs what it always did.

(Which, honestly, is what I learned [the first time I built this blog with Claude](/posts/i-built-this-blog-with-claude-code). The tool doesn't have taste. You do.)

## What I'd do again

- **Ask for directions, not a design.** Three wildly different sites taught me more about what I wanted than one polished one would have.
- **Make the demo real.** I only met the squiggle because the numbers were sitting on real posts.
- **Ask who made it, then ask them.** Claude raised it before I did. Getting a yes from the artist was quicker than drawing a replacement, and a lot better.
- **Ask the boring question early.** "What would it take to ship this properly?" belongs before you fall in love, not after.

Anyway, scroll down. The riders are at the bottom of this page, still heading out.
